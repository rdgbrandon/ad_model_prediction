# Provenance

Read this before you draw a conclusion from this file.

## What the numbers are

**The values in `b200_50MB.h5` are real physical measurements.** They come from
digital holographic microscopy of a driven liquid film, recorded by Orosco,
Connacher and Friend at UC San Diego. Nothing here is simulated, synthesised, or
generated by a model. A law you discover in this data is a law about the
experiment, subject to the band limits described below.

The measurements have been **lossily compressed** — band-limited in space and
time, and quantised — to fit 320.7 GB into 50 MB. That is the only sense in
which they differ from the deposit.

## What was AI-generated

**The tooling.** The two-stage reduction pipeline that produced this file, the
decoder, the example analyses, and this documentation were written with AI
assistance. The pipeline chose how to allocate the byte budget across trials,
what to keep and what to discard.

That is worth knowing for two reasons:

1. **The choice of what to discard was made by a program, not a physicist.** The
   budget was allocated to maximise total coherent wave energy above the
   measured instrument-noise floor. That is a defensible criterion, but it is
   one criterion among several, and it was not validated against any specific
   downstream analysis. If your analysis depends on a band this file does not
   store, the file will quietly not contain it. Check `p.info(trial)` first.
2. **The fidelity table in the README was measured, not estimated** — decoded
   output compared against the original raw frames. Those numbers are empirical.
   The prose around them is not a peer-reviewed claim.

## Reproducibility

The reduction is reproducible from the deposit in principle. The reduction
scripts are not distributed with this package.

## Sample size — read this before you fit anything

There are **21 trials**: one quiescent noise-floor measurement, one static-mode
run, and 19 turbulence runs across 7–350 mW. Two of those (`_42_1`, `_45_1`) are
repeats at the same drive power.

**Twenty-one is the honest sample size at the trial level.** Leave-one-trial-out
gives you 21 folds and very little else. If you fit a model with more than a
handful of parameters against per-trial scalars, you are overfitting, and a
cross-validated score will tell you so.

Each trial does contain ~100,000 frames, so a per-window unit of analysis gives
you thousands of samples instead. If you do that, **group your splits by
trial.** Windows from the same trial share a drive power, an instrument state
and a topography; letting windows from one trial appear in both train and test
will produce an excellent score that means nothing.

Both units are legitimate. They answer different questions. Say which one you
used.
