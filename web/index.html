A computer model guesses how tiny waves behave. We use earlier measurements to say: “This guess is wrong by at least this much.”

02 / WHY THIS MAKES SENSE

Think of a target on a map.

An illustration
01

Draw a region for the possible answer

Imagine a friend says a hidden target is somewhere inside a circle. Earlier measurements help us draw a similar region for the wave measurement.

02

Measure the shortest possible miss

Your guess sits outside the circle. Even the closest point inside is some distance away. That distance is the minimum mistake we can prove.

03

Combine several clues

If several trustworthy clues give different regions, the answer must satisfy them all. Taking the strongest of those regions gives a bound that cannot be weaker than any one of them. On this dataset the strongest single region wins almost every time, so blending them adds next to nothing.

The catch: a wrong region can give a wrong warning. The combined method needs all its rules to hold. We check that using the real test answers afterwards.

More uncertainty means a weaker warning

What changed in the latest experiment?

We kept the uncertainty rule fixed. We fitted additional rules using groups of at least three earlier calibration experiments, then combined their allowed regions. The real test answers were used only afterwards to check the result.

Three reasons to read that gain cautiously.
  • The number cannot go down. The original single rule is still one of the candidates, and we report the strongest candidate. So this comparison can only show an increase or no change. Zero is the floor, not a failure.
  • Blending the regions did almost nothing. — Nearly all of the gain comes from one single group of three experiments: the one that leaves out the lowest calibration power. The weighted-blend machinery is not what produced the improvement.
  • One setting could not improve at all. —

It also needs a stronger assumption. The single rule needed one region to contain the answer. This needs every region to contain it at once. Every tested case passed that stricter check, but it is the same three or four distinct test experiments, so passing is weak evidence either way.

Research detail: earlier checks and the equations
centre = measured ceiling + fitted slope × log-power distance
radius = ∥fitted slope − ½∥ × log-power distance
minimum error = max(distance from guess to centre − radius, 0)

The ½ exponent assumes energy flux scales with drive power. This is not established for this apparatus. Other tested exponents also worked; the data do not identify a unique physical exponent.

For weighted balls, the combined centre is Σwᵢcᵢ and squared radius is Σwᵢrᵢ² − Σwᵢ∥cᵢ − centre∥², with nonnegative weights summing to 1. Feasible weights supply a valid conditional bound even if optimization does not find the best weights.

Why isn’t 3 out of 3 enough?

Getting three coin flips right would not prove you could predict the next hundred. Likewise, three successful experiments leave a lot of uncertainty about new ones.

Under an independent-trial statistical model, 3/3 gives a one-sided 95% lower coverage limit of only 36.8%. Four successes give 47.3%. These experiments share an apparatus and were already inspected, which limits the claim further. A bigger experiment—not more reruns—is needed.

A quick glossary
Model
A mathematical system that learns patterns and makes predictions.
Spectrum
A wave fingerprint: how much activity appears at each frequency.
Frequency
How many times a wave moves up and down each second.
Calibration
Earlier measurements used to build the checking rule.
Lower bound
A minimum value. “The error is at least 5” does not mean it is exactly 5.
Training run / seed
A repeat with a different random starting point. It is not new experimental data.
